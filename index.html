Fictional company / Advertising

AdVista

Uses the invented “elevated stress” label to place the person in a wellness-ad audience across apps.

Ads about stress follow them, potentially exposing a private concern on a shared screen. The label could be wrong, and the person did not agree to this marketing use.

Fictional company / Insurance

HarborCover

Reads the invented “poor recovery” label and removes a wellness discount at renewal.

The person's monthly cost rises because of an unsupported inference. They may not know what data triggered the decision or how to correct it.

Fictional company / Employment

Northstar Work

A manager sees the invented “low energy” label and leaves the worker off an important project.

One speculative profile now affects an opportunity for recognition or advancement, even though the pulse says nothing about job performance.

Fictional company / Reproductive-data marketing

CycleReach

Receives VitalSense's separate cycle predictions and adds the person to a fertility-ad audience.

This example uses a different VitalSense feature, not the pulse. Ads could reveal or misrepresent a private life plan that the person never chose to share with marketers.

Fictional company / Data brokerage

SignalLedger

Packages a VitalSense wellness label with other data and sells a “health interest” audience to clients.

A wrong label can spread to unknown recipients. The person may struggle to discover who has it, challenge it, or make every copy disappear.

Which step should VitalSense have to justify?

Collection → inference → sharing → decision. GDPR principles ask whether each use is lawful, limited to its purpose, necessary, accurate, time-limited, and accountable.
